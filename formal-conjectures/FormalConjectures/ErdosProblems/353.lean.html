    Let $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ be a measurable set with infinite measure. Must $A$ contain the vertices of an isosceles trapezoid of area $1$? What about an isosceles triangle, or a right-angled triangle, or a cyclic quadrilateral, or a convex polygon with congruent sides?

    Koizumi [Ko25] has resolved this question, proving that any set with infinite measure must contain the vertices of an isosceles trapezoid, an isosceles triangle, and a right-angled triangle, all of area $1$.

    This statement formalizes the leading question, for isosceles trapezoids; the remaining configurations are given as variants below. The area of a polygon is taken to be the Lebesgue measure of the convex hull of its vertices.

  4. theorem Erdos353.erdos_353.variants.cyclic_quadrilateral Every measurable $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ with infinite measure contains the vertices of a cyclic quadrilateral of area $1$ sorry line 112 source

    Every measurable $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ with infinite measure contains the vertices of a cyclic quadrilateral of area $1$.

    Kovač and Predojević [KoPr24] have proved that this is true for cyclic quadrilaterals - that is, every set with infinite measure contains four distinct points on a circle such that the quadrilateral determined by these four points has area $1$. The quadrilateral determined by four distinct concyclic points is their convex hull.
